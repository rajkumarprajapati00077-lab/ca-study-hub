Law — how to remember sections

CA Inter Group I · 7 min read · Companies Act, LLP, FEMA

The most common complaint in Law is one line — "I remember the provision, but I forget the section number." And in the exam, writing the section number earns marks. The answer is not rote learning, it is a system — connect the section to its chapter, then to the provision, then to a small story.

Step 1 — Make a chapter-wise section map

Law has 15 chapters. Make a list of the important sections of every chapter — on one page, with the chapter name and its 4–6 important sections. For example:

  • Share Capital & Debentures: Sec 43 (kinds of share capital), Sec 52 (securities premium), Sec 53 (no discount on shares), Sec 54 (sweat equity), Sec 55 (preference shares).
  • Management & Administration: Sec 149 (board of directors), Sec 164 (disqualification), Sec 165 (limit on directorships), Sec 177 (audit committee).
  • Acceptance of Deposits: Sec 73 (prohibition on accepting deposits), Sec 74 (repayment), Sec 76 (public deposits).
  • Accounts & Audit: Sec 128 (books of account), Sec 129 (financial statements), Sec 139 (appointment of auditor), Sec 143 (powers of auditor).

Once this map is made, you will know which section belongs to which chapter — and in the exam, when the chapter comes to mind, the section will follow.

Step 2 — The "Section + Provision + Example" trio

A section number cannot be remembered on its own. Connect every section to three things:

  • Section number: for example, Sec 185.
  • Provision (in one line): "A company cannot give a loan to a director (with some exceptions)."
  • One example: "If a company gives a loan of Rs. 10 lakh to its director, Sec 185 applies."

When you connect a section to an example, a picture forms in your mind — and a picture stays in memory longer than a number. In the exam, when the example comes to mind, the section number will too.

Step 3 — The story method (for remembering a sequence)

Some sections come in a sequence — like the share capital sections (43, 52, 53, 54, 55) or the meeting sections. Tie them into a small story. For example: "The company first created its share capital (43), then took a premium (52), did not issue at a discount (53), gave sweat equity (54), and issued preference shares (55)." The story can be strange — a strange story is remembered better.

Step 4 — 30 minutes of section revision every day

The easiest way to revise Law is 30 minutes a day, only sections. In that time:

  • Open one chapter's section map and write the sections without looking. Mark the ones you forgot.
  • Read the forgotten sections again the same day — and test yourself again the next day.
  • Once a week, go through the section maps of all the chapters together.

30 minutes is not a lot, but doing it every day adds up to 30 hours of section revision in 60 days — and those 30 hours are what will get you the section numbers in the exam.

Look at LLP, GCA and FEMA separately: apart from the Companies Act, the LLP Act 2008, the General Clauses Act 1897, Interpretation of Statutes and FEMA 1999 are also in the syllabus. Don't leave them for the end — keep 2–3 hours a week aside for them. These chapters are small, but they carry marks too.

Writing practice — necessary in Law too

Law is a theory subject, so people only read it and never write it. That is a mistake. In the exam you have to write the answer — the provision, the section, and the conclusion, in the right order. Practise writing at least 2–3 answers every week:

  • Format: first the provision (with the section), then apply it to the facts, then the conclusion.
  • Case-based questions: first identify the issue (which section applies), then write the provision, then the conclusion.
  • Time: practise writing one answer in 8–10 minutes — you will not get more time than that in the exam.

The mistake everyone makes: studying from only the bare act, or only one book. Study Law from one source and revise it again and again. Reading from 3 sources once is worse than reading from 1 source 3 times. Changing the source is not revision, it is confusion.
