Advanced Accounts — how to revise big chapters fast

CA Inter Group I · 7 min read · Amalgamation, Consolidation, Partnership

The biggest problem in Advanced Accounts is that the chapters are large, and every chapter has 10–15 different types of questions. Reading a whole chapter again during revision is not possible. So the method of revision has to be different — format first, adjustments second, questions last.

Step 1 — Remember the "format" of every big chapter

Questions in Advanced Accounts follow a fixed format. If you remember the format, half the question is already done. Write the format of every big chapter on one page:

  • Amalgamation: the calculation of purchase consideration, journal entries, and the format of the transferee company's balance sheet. These three things, on one page.
  • Consolidation (CFS): the format of the consolidated balance sheet — goodwill / capital reserve, minority interest, and where the inter-company adjustments go. If you remember the format, filling in the figures is easy.
  • Partnership: admission, retirement, death and dissolution — each has a different format. Write down the 4–5 key steps of each format.
  • Buyback, Internal Reconstruction, Liquidation: these also follow a fixed flow — what to do first, what next. Put the flow on one page.

The easiest way to remember a format is to write it yourself. Write it once by looking at the book, then write it again without looking. Whatever you can write without looking, you remember.

Step 2 — Make a list of the "golden adjustments"

Every chapter has 4–5 adjustments that keep coming up and where the most marks are lost. Make a list of them:

  • Amalgamation: what is included in purchase consideration and what is not, the treatment of amalgamation expenses, and the transfer of statutory reserves.
  • Consolidation: separating pre-acquisition and post-acquisition profits, unrealised profit on stock, and the calculation of minority interest.
  • Partnership: the treatment of goodwill (on admission / retirement), the effect of revaluation, and the calculation of gainers and sacrificers.

This list is your checklist before the exam — look at it once before solving a question, and the common adjustments will not be missed.

Step 3 — One chapter, 2–3 questions, that's it

In revision, you don't need to solve 10 questions from every chapter. 2–3 good questions are enough — as long as they cover all the important adjustments of the chapter.

  • First question: basic — one with the format and simple adjustments.
  • Second question: tricky — one with 2–3 adjustments together.
  • Third question (if there is time): from a past paper or MTP — like the real exam.

Solve the questions against the clock. Sit with a watch even in revision — in the exam you get limited time for every question, and without practice you never learn how long a question actually takes.

Step 4 — A one-page summary for every chapter

Make a one-page summary for every big chapter. It should have:

  • The chapter's format (short, in points).
  • The golden adjustments (4–5, one line each).
  • One formula or rule you always forget.
  • The name of one question you solved that covered everything.

This one-page summary will help you in the last days — you won't need to read the whole chapter again; reading this one page will be enough.

Time budget: a fast revision of one big chapter (like Amalgamation) should take 2–3 hours — 30 minutes for the format, 30 minutes for adjustments, 1 hour for questions, 30 minutes for the summary. If one chapter is taking 5 hours, you are not revising — you are studying it again. Go back to the format.

Don't forget the small chapters

While focusing on the big chapters, the small ones (like Underwriting, ESOP, or the basics of Accounting Standards) get left out. Small chapters can give more marks in less time — set aside 1 hour each for them separately. One small chapter that you know fully can give more marks than half of a big chapter.

The mistake everyone makes: only reading and understanding in revision — not writing. Advanced Accounts is remembered by writing. A question you "understood" without writing will get stuck when you try to write it in the exam. In every revision, solve at least 2 questions fully, in writing.
